What Staple is for, and what to do first

Staple answers two questions that decide whether a repair is finished on the first visit. Which parts should the technician's truck carry for tomorrow's booked calls, and which technician should take each call? When the stock lives on the trucks, those are one decision, so Staple scores them together.

Every number in it comes from repair history I invented: {{jobs}} jobs over {{days}} days from {{start}}, across {{types}} appliance types and {{techs}} technicians. The last {{test_days}} days are held back. Each method learns from the earlier days only, and is then scored on the held-back days as if they had not happened yet. That is a back-test, and it is the same test I would run first on real history.

What to do first. The report is one screen with two sides. On the left is the plan for one invented day: read the three steps across the top, then the to-do list, which says what each team does before {{day_start}}. On the right is the proof: that day against the current rules, then the evidence from all {{test_days}} held-back days. The next section walks through every part of the screen.

Hover over anything with a dotted underline, or anything that turns your pointer into a question mark. The hint shows the figures behind it and the query they come from. A blue ? opens this manual at the word it explains. Under every chart, "Show the numbers" opens the table the chart is drawn from.

How to read the plan screen

The report is one screen with two sides, and on a desktop each side scrolls on its own, so the page itself never does. The left side is the plan for one invented day. The right side is the proof that the plan beats the current rules. Choosing a technician anywhere on the left lights up their route, their row and their load sheet, and shows their card on the right. Every page opens light. The Dark mode button at the top switches it, and your browser remembers the choice.

Across the top

Three steps say what Staple does, in order: Claude codes every booking, Staple plans the trucks, and people act before {{day_start}}. Under them, the day buttons pick one of the most recent {{week}} held-back days. They are not picked by hand. A ✚ marks a technician who called out sick and a ⚠ a truck that broke down, so the days with surprises show before a click. The page opens on the most recent day on which a sick call and a breakdown both moved calls to other trucks.

The left side, top to bottom

  • The day's four tiles: booked calls, the share expected fixed on the first visit, repeat visits avoided, and parts moved overnight. The first three compare the plan with the current rules on the same day; the last counts the plan's own moves.
  • AI at booking: how many bookings Claude coded at once, and a card for every booking it was unsure of. A card shows the customer's words, Claude's first reading with its confidence, the one question it asked, the answers offered with the one taken in yellow, and the code the call runs on. Where the answer changed the carry list, the card shows what the first guess would have loaded and what the truck loads now.
  • To do before {{day_start}}: every action, grouped by who takes it. The next section lists every kind. Clicking an item lights up its truck and opens, on the right, the evidence behind that kind of action.
  • {{sick_by}} and after: each sick call and breakdown, every call it moved, from whom to whom, and its first-time fix chance before and after. A moved call can use only the parts its new truck already carries.
  • Routes and the board: the map of the {{zones}} zones beside a row for each technician from {{day_start}} to {{day_end}}. Each block is a call, shaded by its first-time fix chance, on the scale the legend above the board shows. The switch shows the plan as made at night or the day as it ran. On the day as it ran, a sick technician's row is hatched, a broken truck's row stops at a ⚠ marker, a moved call is outlined and tagged with the technician it came from, and on the map a dashed line joins it to its new truck. A yellow corner marks a call Claude asked about, a hatched block a call added on the day, a red underline a visit missing a needed part, and ▲ a call escalated to urgent.
  • Load sheet: the chosen truck's list for the parts room tonight. Each row says put on or take off, the part and its made-up cost, its worth, and the booked calls behind it with each one's chance of needing it. Under it, the parts kept aboard: those tomorrow's calls may need, and a count by appliance of the stock kept for unplanned calls.

The right side

First, the day against the current rules, with both replays on Claude's codes and the same surprises. Then the chosen technician's card: their appliances, how often they fix each first time, and how fast. Then the evidence from all {{test_days}} held-back days, which runs on the call-taker's booked codes so it measures the planning apart from the reading. A card that leads to an action ends with what a fleet does with it. The last two cards say how a fleet would adopt Staple, and what this does and does not show.

What the numbers mean

The scorecard across all held-back days

It sits on the right side, under the day's comparison and the technician's card, and runs on the call-taker's booked codes. Each row is one measure of the whole plan, replayed over every held-back day with the same made-up sick calls and breakdowns. It gives the measure under the current rules, under Staple, the difference, and the control.

First-time fix is the share of all held-back calls fixed on the first visit. A call fixes first time only when the truck holds every part it needs, and the technician's own success rate on that appliance then decides it. A call rolled to tomorrow counts as not fixed.

Parts-caused reschedules counts the calls whose truck lacked a needed part, for every hundred calls served. Each of them needs a repeat visit whatever the technician's skill.

Fill rate is the share of the part lines those calls needed that were on the truck. A call that needs two parts is two lines. So one missing part costs one line here, and the whole call in the row above.

Parts cycle time is the days a repair waits for its parts, spread over every repair. The chart on how long a repair waits adds the wait of a repair that does wait.

Inventory return is the repeat-visit dollars a year that a truck's stock avoids, per dollar of stock aboard, at {{repeat_visit}} a repeat visit. A call counts when it needed a part and every part was aboard. That dollar figure is made up, and the threshold table names every made-up value.

The current rules send each call to the nearest qualified technician, driving today's fixed truck stock. The control is Staple with the history behind its part odds shuffled, so it knows what each appliance usually needs and nothing finer. Its column gives its own figure and the share of the difference it keeps. What it keeps needs only that knowledge. The rest comes from the history's links to model family and problem.

The charts

Where repeat visits come from counts, per 100 jobs of each appliance, the repeat visits caused by a part missing from the truck. Its table adds the share of each appliance's repeat visits that a missing part caused. The rest failed on the technician's success rate.

How long a repair waits for its parts gives the parts cycle time: the days from a visit that lacked a part to the repeat visit that brings it. The first row is the held-back history as recorded. Each other row replays one way of handing out calls, where a repair that lacks a part waits the made-up lead times. The bar spreads the wait over every repair, so it falls when fewer repairs wait. The table adds the share of repairs that wait, the days one of them waits, and the share of those waits that are on a back-ordered part.

Which parts to carry plots, for carry lists of {{ks}} parts, the share of held-back jobs whose every needed part was on the list. The horizontal line is today's trucks, which carry far more than the longest list's {{max_k}} parts. The list's job is to top the truck up for the day, not to replace it. The table's leaked column scores the predictor again with the held-back jobs already in its history. The gap between the two is what holding those days back is worth.

What to load tonight ranks each way of loading a truck by its cost a truck-day: repeat visits for jobs the truck could not cover, parts moved on or off overnight, and the cost of money tied up in stock. Lower is better. Each bar's label also gives the share of jobs covered. Some rows test the balanced truck: loaded from shuffled history, held to the space today's stock uses, and with one move priced at {{move_steps}} where the other rows price it at {{move}}.

Who takes the call replays every held-back day under each way of handing out calls, with the same made-up sick calls, breakdowns and priority changes. The bar is calls fixed on the first visit; the label adds the cost a call. Two rows are controls: one shuffles the technicians' skills, the other shuffles the history behind the parts odds the dispatcher reads.

Claude coding the bookings compares six readings of the customer's words: Claude, Claude after its question with ideal replies and with random replies, the booked code, a plain keyword matcher, and a control in which Claude's answers are shuffled between bookings. Each bar is how often the {{shown_k}}-part carry list built from the reading covered the job, and its label adds how often the reading agrees with the booked code. The row with ideal replies is an upper bound: the customer who answers Claude's question is simulated and always picks the answer that matches the booking. The row with random replies is the lower end, and a real customer lands between them. The keyword matcher picks the code whose label shares the most words with the customer's. It is a floor to beat, not a picture of how any booking screen works. The invented customers say each problem only a few ways, so every score is over that many distinct descriptions, and the card gives the count.

How often Claude is right, by its confidence groups Claude's first readings by the confidence it stated. Each bar is the share of those readings that match the booked code. A reader that can be trusted is right at least as often as the confidence it states, and seldom right where it says it is unsure. Below {{blend_below}} Staple does not run on the code alone, and Claude asks its question. A band under that line that is right as often as the bands above it means the line is set cautiously.

Back-orders to chase first lists each part the centers cannot ship, with the calls expected to need it before it arrives, how many of those have no truck nearby holding one, and the dollars at risk.

Each gain against its control draws, for every control, the share of the gain it keeps. A control that keeps under {{falls_back}} has fallen back, so that gain came from the link the control broke. A long bar is a gain that came from something plainer, and the card that owns the gain says what.

How each computed figure is made

ColumnHow it is computed
oddsFor one booking, the share of past jobs like it that needed each part. "Like it" means the same model family and problem, leaning on the appliance and problem, then the appliance alone, where history is thin.
coverageThe share of jobs whose every needed part was on the list or truck. One missing part fails the whole job, the way one short line holds a whole order.
first_time_fix_pctExpected first-time fixes per held-back call: covered times the technician's success rate on that appliance, with a rolled call counted as zero.
cost_per_callDrive and repair minutes at {{minute}}, plus one minus the fix rate, times {{repeat_visit}}.
cost_a_truck_dayRepeat visits at {{repeat_visit}}, plus {{move}} for each part moved on or off overnight, plus each part's cost times {{holding_rate}} a year, per day.
roiRepeat-visit dollars a year that a truck's stock avoids, per dollar of stock on board. A job counts as one repeat visit avoided when it needed a part and every part was aboard. A job that needed none is left out, because an empty truck covers it.
gain_kept_pctFor a control: its figure less the figure without the method, over the method's figure less the same, from the three figures as shown. Under {{falls_back}}, the control has fallen back.
leaked_pctCoverage for the predictor with the held-back jobs already in its history. No scored method is built that way; the column only measures what the hold-out is worth.
expected_callsFor a back-ordered part: its share of its appliance's past calls, times that appliance's calls a day in the same month of past years, times the days until it ships.
expected_stuckExpected calls in zones with no holding truck based within {{nearby}} minutes, less the calls the holders themselves usually take there.
dollars_at_riskExpected stuck calls times {{repeat_visit}}.
actual_calls, actual_stuckWhat the held-back days then brought before the part shipped, for comparison with the forecast.
back_test1 on the first day a back-order is listed, when it ships inside the held-back days. The forecast is scored on these rows, so each back-order counts once and every call that came is counted.
fill rateOn the scorecard: the part lines aboard over the part lines needed, across the calls a plan served. A call needing two parts is two lines.
inventory returnOn the scorecard: the calls a truck-day that needed a part and had every one aboard, times {{repeat_visit}}, times the days in a year, over the dollars of stock aboard a truck-day. Every truck counts on every day, working or not.
control_keeps_pctOn the scorecard: the control's figure less the current rules', over Staple's figure less the same, from the three figures as shown.
repairs_waiting_pctRepairs whose truck lacked a needed part, over every repair counted.
days_when_waiting, days_a_repairDays from the visit that lacked a part to the repeat visit. In the history row they are the recorded days. In a replayed row each waiting repair counts the average over every order day and lead time the made-up table allows, and a part on back-order waits first for the day a center can ship it. The first figure divides by the repairs that wait, the second by every repair.
waits_on_backorder_pctOf the repairs that wait, the share lacking a part no center could ship on the day of the visit.

How the data is handled

Everything is invented by one seeded generator (seed {{seed}}) and written to one SQLite file on the machine that runs it. The same seed writes the same rows every time, so every figure reproduces exactly. No real company, customer, part number or technician appears anywhere.

The report, this manual, the methods page and the code languages page are static pages. They load nothing from the network: no fonts, no scripts, no tracking. The published copy is the same file, built on the author's machine and checked against a fresh build before it is committed.

The AI step's answers were produced once by {{model}} and ship in the repository, so a run needs no key and sends nothing anywhere. Only python run.py --live calls the Claude API, with the reader's own key, and it sends the made-up booking words and problem labels and nothing else.

What Staple cannot tell you

  • Whether the gains hold on real history. The history is invented, so every figure shows the method working, not the size of a real gain.
  • Real costs. The repeat-visit, handling, holding and minute costs are stand-ins. The ranking of loads can change with real costs; the break-even figure in the run says where.
  • Quantities. A truck that holds a part is taken to hold enough of it. Two calls on one route needing the same part are not modeled.
  • Routes. Drive time is zone to zone on a grid, not road routing, and the night plan places calls one at a time rather than solving the whole day at once.
  • Why a repair failed when the parts were there. The history records the technician's success rate, not wrong diagnoses or customer no-shows.
  • Whether the AI reads real customers this well. The customer phrasings are a small invented set, and the simulated customer answers the follow-up question with the booked problem.

The methods page lists the corners the code cuts on purpose, each with the upgrade that would lift it.

Running it yourself

Python {{python}} or newer is the only requirement; no packages are installed. From a copy of the repository:

python run.py          # builds the made-up world, every result, the metric tables and these pages into out/
python check.py        # every check, each with a control that proves it can fail
python run.py --live   # the AI step asked afresh, with your own ANTHROPIC_API_KEY

Open out/index.html in a browser. The {{queries}} metric queries are plain SQL files in sql/metrics/; each runs on its own against out/staple.db.
