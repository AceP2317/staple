Design principles

A known truth, planted on purpose. The history is generated, so the true problem behind every booking and the noise in it are known. That lets each method be scored against the truth as well as against what a real history would record. A real history records only the booked code.

A temporal hold-out. The last {{test_days}} days are held back. Every count, share and rate a method uses is read from the earlier days (split = 'train'), and the method is then scored on the held-back days. No held-back fact reaches a method. One row breaks that rule on purpose and is never a result: the leak test scores the predictor with the held-back jobs in its history, to measure what the hold-out is worth.

A control for every gain. Each claimed gain has a control that breaks the link the gain should come from: shuffled history, shuffled technician skills, shuffled AI answers, random follow-up replies. The share of a gain its control keeps is:

kept = (with the control − without the method) / (with the method − without the method)

A control falls back when it keeps under {{falls_back}}. A gain is credited to a link only where its control falls back. controls.sql lists every control with the share it kept, and the report names the ones that do not fall back and what those gains rest on. Where a check could pass without looking, it also runs a planted input that must make it fail.

One seed, one source. Seed {{seed}} reproduces every row. Every figure on an evidence card is read from the result of a SQL query in sql/metrics/, or is one of the made-up values in the assumptions table. The plan, the day's comparison and the technician cards are the dispatch replay's own record of the week. Every number on this page and in the instruction manual is read from the code at build time, and the check wording refuses a numeral typed into either.

The invented world

Source: staple/world.py. Values set inside a function are quoted here with that function's name.

Calendar and volume

{{jobs}} jobs, each on a day drawn uniformly from {{days}} days starting {{start}}. Jobs on the last {{test_days}} days form the test split.

Appliances, problems and parts

{{types}} appliance types, each with {{problems_min}} to {{problems_max}} problem codes and {{parts_min}} to {{parts_max}} parts. Each part carries a cost, a size in truck space units, and a lock flag; a locked part may ride only with a certified technician. Each problem carries customer phrasings and a weight for each part it can need. Furnace calls follow the seasons (_season_pick): a furnace call's weight is {{furnace_winter}} from {{winter_months}} and {{furnace_summer}} from {{summer_months}}. The rest of the year it weighs the same as every other type.

Model families

{{families}} families per type. The {{rare_families}} newest of each type enter the field recently, so their history is thin on purpose. Each family has a weak part, drawn {{weak_part}} times as often wherever the problem can need it (pick_parts).

Parts a job needs

Per job (pick_parts): with probability {{no_part}} no part (an adjustment); with probability {{one_part}} one part drawn from the problem's weights; otherwise two, the second drawn from the remaining parts' weights.

Planted noise

  • Misdescribed, {{misdescribed}} of jobs: the booked code is a different problem of the same appliance than the true one.
  • Patternless, {{random_failure}} of jobs: the needed part is drawn with equal weight across the appliance's parts, ignoring the problem; the family's weak part still counts {{weak_part}} times.
  • Vague words, {{vague}} of jobs: the customer's phrasing fits no single problem.

Technicians and trucks

{{techs}} technicians (_build_techs). Level is {{levels}} with weights {{level_weights}}, setting a base success rate of {{level_rates}} and a repair-time multiplier of {{level_times}}. Each technician is a specialist with probability {{specialist}}. A specialist covers {{specialist_types}} appliance types, with {{specialist_rate}} added to the rate and {{specialist_time}} times the repair time; the rest cover every type. Each type then varies the rate by up to ±{{rate_spread}} and the time by up to ±{{time_spread}}, and no rate passes {{top_rate}}. Every senior is certified, and each mid with probability {{mid_certified}}.

Each technician drives one truck of {{capacity}} space units. Today's stock is the nationally most-used parts of each type the technician covers, taken one rank at a time across those types. A part that would take the load past {{stock_fill}} of the space is skipped, so a truck ends at or under that mark. A locked part is skipped on an uncertified truck.

Geography

{{zones}} zones on a {{grid}} grid, {{zone_miles}} miles apart. Drive time (travel_minutes) is {{inside_zone}} minutes inside a zone, and between zones {{between_zones}} minutes plus {{minutes_a_mile}} minutes a mile between zone centers.

What history recorded

The technician who took each historical job (_history_tech) covers its appliance, chosen with weight 1 / (1 + drive minutes from home). The job was fixed on the first visit only when every needed part was on that technician's truck and a uniform draw fell below the technician's rate.

Where a needed part was not on the truck, history also records two days (_build_waits): the day the part was ordered and the day of the repeat visit. The order goes in on the visit day, or the next morning with probability {{order_next_day}}. Each ordered part then takes {{lead_times}} to arrive, and a part on back-order waits first for the day a center can ship it. The repeat visit falls on the day the last part arrives. Both days are drawn from a stream of their own, so adding them moved no other row: the check history-dates builds the world with and without them and compares every other column.

Distribution centers

The {{centers}} distribution centers restock trucks between workdays (_build_centers). Across the held-back days each part goes on back-order with probability {{backorder_starts}} a day, for {{backorder_days}} days. No center can ship it until the day it ships, and from that day any truck can load it again.

The predictor

Source: staple/predict.py. For a booking with appliance a, model family f and booked problem q, it estimates for each part p of a the probability that the job needs p. Training jobs only; problems are the booked codes, because real history records nothing else.

{{EX_predictor}}

The family estimate is the answer. Each level is a pseudo-count estimate whose prior is the wider level's estimate with strength P, which is the posterior mean under a Beta prior. With a handful of real jobs the wider level dominates; with thousands the family's own history decides. The carry list of length k is the k parts with the highest estimate, ties broken by part name.

The national top-parts list, standing in for today's stocking logic, ranks the appliance's parts by c_a(p) alone, whatever the problem.

The back-test

Source: staple/backtest.py. Each held-back job is scored on whether its carry list held every part it needed.

{{EX_backtest}}

A job needing no part is covered by any list. Today's trucks are scored by the recorded flag of every needed part being on the truck. Variants scored at every k:

  • predictor, as above;
  • national top parts, the baseline;
  • no lean on wider groups, the predictor with P = 0, which isolates what the lean does for thin families;
  • shuffled-history control: within each appliance type, each training job's set of needed parts is dealt to a random other job of the same type (seed {{shuffle_seed}}). The type-level shares survive; every link to family and problem is destroyed. The control must fall to the national baseline, or the gain is not coming from the links.
  • leak test: the predictor with the held-back jobs, answers included, already in its history. No scored method is built this way. Its gap to the predictor is what holding the days back is worth. The gap is small here because the invented failure rates never change over time; on real history, where models and parts change, it is the gap to watch.

Coverage is also cut by planted noise (none, misdescribed, patternless) and by family age (newest against established) at k = {{shown_k}}.

The truck-load replay

Source: staple/load.py. A truck-day is one technician's held-back jobs on one day. Each truck's days are replayed in date order, and each night's load starts from the previous night's load. On the first night it starts from today's stock. Each held-back job is independently marked unplanned with probability {{unplanned}} (seed {{unplanned_seed}}); an unplanned call is added or moved on the day, so no night-before list sees it.

Cost of a truck-day

With repeat-visit cost R = {{repeat_visit}}, handling M = {{move}} a part moved, and holding rate h = {{holding_rate}} a year:

repeat = (jobs not covered by the load) · R handling = |load △ before| · M holding = Σ_(p ∈ load) cost_p · h / 365 total = repeat + handling + holding

Every figure is averaged per truck-day. Stock value on board is reported, but it is money tied up, not spent; only its holding cost enters the total.

Filling a truck

Parts are taken in descending score. A part goes on when it is not already aboard, the truck may carry it, and it fits the space left. A truck may carry a locked part only if its technician is certified. A back-ordered part cannot be loaded, so a truck carries one only if it was aboard the night before, and it stays aboard until the part ships.

Policies

  • Today's trucks: the fixed stock, every day.
  • Today's stock plus the day's lists: the fixed stock, then the union of the booked calls' top-k lists, each part scored by its highest odds on the route. Run for k = {{ks}}.
  • The day's lists only: the same lists into an empty truck.
  • Every part that fits for the day's types: the top-up with every part on the list.
  • Balanced truck: each part's worth for tomorrow, net of what carrying it costs, per unit of space.

The balanced truck

For technician t, unplanned share U = {{unplanned}}, and odds o_j(p) from the predictor for each booked call j on tomorrow's route:

s_t(p) = U · (training calls of type(p) for t / training days of t) · c_type(p) / n_type · R w_t(p) = s_t(p) + Σ_(booked j) o_j(p) · R net_t(p) = w_t(p) − cost_p · h / 365 + (M if p is aboard tonight, else −M) score(p) = net_t(p) / size_p, kept only where net_t(p) > 0

The truck is filled from empty in descending score. A part already aboard is worth one move more than one that must be loaded. So a part stays on the truck while it earns its space, and permanent stock emerges from demand rather than being set.

Choosing a list length and the break-even

For each list policy, the recommended length is the shortest whose total is within {{lean_tolerance}} a truck-day of that policy's cheapest.

{{EX_loads}}

The repeat-visit cost at which the chosen top-up stops paying is:

R* = (Δhandling + Δholding) / (Δrepeat / R) (top-up against today's trucks)

A part is called permanent when it is aboard on at least {{permanent}} of a truck's days. Inventory ROI is the jobs per truck-day that needed a part and had every one aboard, times R times the days in a year, divided by the stock value on board. A job that needed no part is left out, because an empty truck covers it.

Controls and tests

The top-up and the balanced truck are rerun with carry lists from the shuffled-history predictor. A second shuffle with another seed measures how far two shuffles differ by chance. The balanced truck's edge over its shuffled control must exceed three times that difference. That control does not fall back. The shuffle keeps what each appliance usually needs, so a truck loaded from it is still loaded for tomorrow's appliances, and the report gives the share of the saving it keeps.

Two more replays test the balanced truck. Today's stock stops at {{stock_fill}} of the truck's space and the balanced truck may fill all of it, so one replay holds each truck to the space its own stock uses today. The price of one move is made up, so the truck is also replayed at {{move_steps}} a move, planning by that price and paying it. At a price high enough it moves nothing and becomes today's truck, and the check controls-shown holds it to that.

Availability and the back-order flag

Each carry-list part gets the first mark that applies, for the job's technician and zone:

    {{MARK_ORDER}}

The back-order flag sums, over every part of the booking's ranked list, the odds of the parts marked back-ordered.

{{EX_availability}}

Because the sum is a probability, its total over all held-back bookings forecasts how many will in fact need a stuck part. The check compares that forecast with the count, and it fails when the forecast misses by more than a quarter of the count.

The fleet back-order list

Source: staple/backorders.py. Which booking will need a stuck part cannot be known in advance, but how many will can, so the action is fleet-wide. A back-order starts on one day and ships on another, so the list is made again for every held-back day: the days left count down, and a part drops off the day it ships.

{{EX_backorders}}

Demand is seasonal, so the month matters: a furnace part on back-order in winter carries far more exposure than the same part in spring. Each day's list is ranked by dollars at risk. The back-test scores each back-order once, on the first day it is listed, and only when it ships inside the held-back days, so every call that came is counted. It counts the held-back calls that needed the part before it shipped, and how many of them were marked back-ordered. The check accepts the forecast when it sits within three Poisson spreads of what came. It also requires that a forecast three times too high fails.

The AI step

Source: staple/triage.py. Model {{model}} at effort {{effort}}.

What Claude sees

One request per appliance type. It holds the appliance, its problem codes with their labels, and every distinct customer description of that type, numbered. Claude never sees the generator's phrasing table, the booked code or the true code. The system prompt and the request, verbatim:

{{SYSTEM}}
{{TEMPLATE}}

The reply is a JSON array of objects with index, code and confidence. The whole reply is refused if any description is missing, any code is outside the appliance's list, or any confidence falls outside 0 to 1.

How answers are produced and cached

Answers ship in data/triage-cache.json, keyed by a prompt identity: the first {{id_length}} hex characters of SHA-256 over the model, effort, system prompt and template. A changed prompt cannot reuse an old cache. The cache was built with the Claude Code command line in its non-interactive mode, with no tools and no saved session, and with any API key removed from its environment so it runs on a subscription. python run.py --live sends the same requests to the Messages API over HTTPS with the reader's key, API version {{api_version}}.

From a reading to a carry list

A reading is a code q with confidence c. When c ≥ {{blend_below}}, the carry list is the predictor's list for q. When it is lower, the odds lean on the appliance's overall shares:

o'(p) = c · o_q(p) + (1 − c) · c_a(p) / n_a

A booking with no code gets the national top-parts list.

The follow-up question

For every description Claude is unsure of (confidence below {{blend_below}}), a second request asks for one question to put to the customer, verbatim:

{{FOLLOWUP_SYSTEM}}
{{FOLLOWUP_TEMPLATE}}

Questions ship in data/followup-cache.json, keyed the same way. In the replay, the simulated customer picks the offered answer whose code is the booked code. When no offered answer fits, the lean reading stands. A real customer answers less surely than that, so the score after the question is an upper bound, and the report labels it one. The control replies at random, seeded by the job number, and must lower coverage; it is the lower end, and a real customer lands between the two.

{{EX_ai}}

The keyword matcher it is compared with

The matcher is the simplest reading there is, a floor for Claude to beat. It is no claim about how any booking screen works. Words are lowercased, cut to letters, stripped of the suffixes "ing", "er", "es", "ed" and "s" when at least three letters remain, and cleared of eight stop words. The code whose label shares the most words with the customer's wins, with ties going to the alphabetically first. No shared word means no code.

What is measured

Agreement with the booked code, the only target a real history allows, and agreement with the true code, known here only because the data is invented. The {{shown_k}}-part carry list's coverage is measured too, which is what the reading is for. The control deals Claude's answers to other descriptions of the same appliance (seed {{answer_seed}}). It falls toward chance, which shows the agreement comes from reading the words.

What the sample is

The invented customers say each problem only a few ways: an opener, one of three phrasings, and sometimes "since yesterday". So the held-back bookings hold a few hundred distinct descriptions, and a score over those bookings is a score over that many readings, weighted by how often each is said. ai_step.sql returns the count, and the report states it beside the scores.

Calibration

Claude's first readings are grouped by the confidence it stated, in bands from each lower edge up to the next. For each band ai_calibration.sql returns the bookings, the distinct phrasings, and how often the reading matches the booked code and the true code. The check triage-honest counts the bands again from the bookings and holds Claude to what it states. In every band the reading must match the booked code at least as often as the band's lower edge, and in the lowest band less often than its upper edge. Shuffled answers must break the first rule. The line at {{blend_below}} is a design choice. A band under it that is right as often as the bands above it shows the line is cautious, and the report says so when that happens. Staple does not move the line to fit, because it would then be tuned on the jobs it is scored on.

The dispatch replay

Source: staple/dispatch.py. A working day is {{day}} minutes, {{day_start}} to {{day_end}}. Repair minutes for an average technician are {{REPAIR}}, multiplied by the technician's own time multiplier for that appliance.

The day's surprises

Drawn once per held-back day from seed {{dispatch_seed}} plus the day's index, and shared by every policy. Each technician is sick with probability {{sick}}. Each truck breaks down with probability {{breakdown}}, at a time drawn uniformly between {{breaks_from}} and {{breaks_to}}. Each call escalates to urgent with probability {{escalate}} or eases to low with probability {{ease}}. These problems are urgent: {{URGENT}}. These are low: {{LOW}}. Every other call sits between them, and the day's changes apply once the day starts.

The cost of sending technician t to call j

Each candidate's cost adds the minutes the visit takes to a repeat visit, weighted by the chance this technician and truck fail the call.

{{EX_dispatch}}

The technician's success rate in that cost is learned, never given. For each technician and appliance Staple counts the training visits that had every part aboard and how many of them fixed the repair. It then mixes in {{skill_pull}} jobs at the appliance's average rate, so a technician with few visits is taken as near average until their own record says otherwise. The rates the made-up world was written from are read only to score what a plan would have fixed. check.py dispatch-blind scrambles them after the history exists and requires every visit to stay where it was. The technician's speed is still given to the rule, because the history records no repair minutes.

At night a part is available when t's truck may carry it, because the center loads overnight. During the day it is available only when it is on the truck now. A technician is a candidate only when they cover the appliance, are working, and their day still fits the call. Fit is always judged on the true repair times, so a wrong belief can never overbook a day. Only the choice between candidates uses the beliefs.

The night plan: regret insertion

Booked calls are planned in priority bands, urgent first. Within a band every call's cost is computed for every candidate. Its regret is the second-lowest cost minus the lowest, or infinite when only one candidate remains. The call with the highest regret is placed with its cheapest candidate first, ties going to the lower job number. Only that technician's costs are then recomputed. A call with no candidate rolls to tomorrow. Booking order would instead fill the best technicians' days with whichever calls came first.

The day as it happens

  1. Each route's truck is loaded overnight by the balanced rule from its planned calls, or keeps today's stock under the baseline.
  2. At {{sick_by}} sick technicians drop out, and their calls are placed one by one with the daytime rule.
  3. The day then runs in clock order. Each unplanned call comes in at its own minute, drawn uniformly between {{day_start}} and {{same_day_until}}, and is placed with the daytime rule.
  4. A breakdown at minute τ takes the truck out, and every call on it starting at or after τ is placed again.
  5. No technician sets out for a call before the minute it came in or was moved to them, so a technician may wait between calls. A wait is not counted as driving.
  6. At day's end, while a route runs past {{day}} minutes, its lowest-priority call, the latest first, rolls to tomorrow.

Scoring

covered(visit) = N(j) ⊆ truck(t) fix(visit) = covered · r_t,a (the true rate) fixes per call = Σ fix / all calls, a rolled call scoring 0 cost a call = Σ (drive + repair) · {{minute}} / served + (1 − Σ fix / served) · R

Policies and controls

  • Nearest technician, today's stock and nearest technician, balanced loads: drive time alone.
  • Skills only: skills, with every part assumed available.
  • Parts only: parts, with every technician believed average: the fleet's mean rate and an unchanged repair time.
  • Staple: skills and parts.
  • Shuffled skills (control): each technician-appliance pair reads another pair's rate and time (seed {{skills_seed}}); who covers what stays true. It must lose at least half the skill gain over parts only.
  • Staple on shuffled history (control): the truck loads and the dispatcher's parts judgment both use shuffled-history odds. It is the scorecard's control, and the scorecard gives what it keeps of each measure's gain.
  • Shuffled-history parts odds (control): the dispatcher's parts judgment uses shuffled-history odds; the trucks still load on the real ones. It stays within a point of Staple, so it does not fall back. At night any truck can load anything but locked or back-ordered parts, so the parts judgment does most of its work during the day, on calls that come in or are moved and can use only what a truck already carries. Trucks are loaded by appliance, and the shuffle keeps what each appliance usually needs. The check measures both halves of that reason: odds that know only the appliance must score within half a point of the shuffled ones, and odds handed over for same-day calls alone must keep more than half the gain.

The checks also require zero rule breaks: no call served or rolled other than once, no sick technician working, no broken truck working after its breakdown, no route past {{day_end}}. A control may roll at most one percent more calls than Staple, so no control can win by overbooking.

The buyer's four actions

Source: buyer.prices and buyer.score. Each back-ordered part on each held-back day is one case for the buyer, who takes one of four actions on it. The engine prices all four in expected dollars from the back-order list's own forecast, so a price uses training only.

  • Send anyway: the calls go out as booked. The price is the calls expected to be stuck before the part ships, at {{repeat_visit}} each.
  • Expedite: a fee of {{expedite}}, and the part ships in {{expedite_days}} days. Only the calls stuck in those days still risk a repeat visit.
  • Move one: a truck that holds the part gives it to a truck that holds none, at {{move}} for each of the two moves. The engine tries every such pair and keeps the one that leaves the fewest calls stuck, counting the calls the giving truck can no longer cover. It cannot be done when no truck holds the part.
  • Rebook: that day's booked calls flagged for the part are moved past the ship day, at {{wait_day}} for each call and day of waiting. Those calls no longer risk a repeat visit for the part. It cannot be done when no booked call is waiting on it.
{{EX_buyer}}

A decider is anything that names an action for a case. It is charged the price of the action it named, and its score is the dollars that costs over the cheapest action. An action that cannot be carried out is charged as sending anyway, and counted. Two fixed rules are scored beside the engine's own arithmetic: sending anyway every time, and expediting every time. The control takes the cheapest action for another case's facts, so its choice no longer rests on the case it is made for. The check buyer-scorer works the four prices again apart from the engine, recounts the waiting calls from the bookings, and tries every move again from the tables.

Parts cycle time

Source: world.expected_wait, called by the dispatch replay, and sql/metrics/parts_cycle_time.sql. Parts cycle time is the days a repair waits for its parts: from the visit that lacked one to the repeat visit that brings it. The history records both days, so its row of the query is a count. A replay has no recorded days. So each replayed visit that lacks a part is given the mean wait under the rule the history was written by: the order goes in on the visit day or the next morning, each missing part takes a lead time from the day a center can ship it, and the repeat visit waits for the last part to arrive.

{{EX_cycle}}

The check cycle-time holds that mean to the history: over every recorded wait, the mean and the recorded days must agree within the spread chance allows. Every row of the query draws on the same lead times. So a way of handing out calls can change how many repairs wait and which parts they wait for, never how long a supplier takes.

The scorecard

Source: sql/metrics/scorecard.sql, over the dispatch replay's table. The scorecard gives five measures of the whole plan across the held-back days, each under three plans: the current rules, Staple, and Staple's control. Every figure is counted in the one replay, on the same calls and the same surprises.

  • First-time fix: expected first-time fixes over every held-back call. A visit counts its technician's success rate when the truck holds every part the call needs, and nothing when it does not. A rolled call counts nothing.
  • Parts-caused reschedules: of the calls served, those whose truck lacked a needed part, for every hundred.
  • Fill rate: of the part lines the calls served needed, the share aboard the truck that came. A call that needs two parts is two lines.
  • Parts cycle time: the mean wait of the section above, summed over the calls served and divided by all of them.
  • Inventory return: the calls a truck-day that needed a part and had every one aboard, times {{repeat_visit}}, times the days in a year, over the dollars of stock aboard a truck-day. Every truck counts on every day, working or not, because its stock is held either way.

The control is the row {{shuffled_history}}. Its predictor is trained on the shuffled history of the back-test's control, and both the truck loads and the dispatcher's parts judgment read its odds. Those odds still know what each appliance usually needs. So the share of a difference the control keeps is what that knowledge alone is worth, and the rest is what the links to model family and problem add. The difference and that share are worked from the figures as shown. The check scorecard counts the replay's figures again from its recorded visits and loads.

The week's plan

Source: staple/plan.py. Nothing in it plans anything. Every route, load and move on the plan screen is what the dispatch replay decided, recorded as it decided it.

Which days

The most recent {{week}} held-back days. Unplanned calls are marked over the whole held-back period exactly as the {{test_days}}-day run marks them, then the week is kept.

Claude's codes

Each booking's code is replaced by Claude's reading of its words, from the same saved answers the AI step scores. Where Claude's confidence is below {{blend_below}} and a saved follow-up question exists, the simulated customer picks the offered answer whose code is the booked code, as in the AI step. Where no offered answer fits, Claude's top guess stands, and the call goes on the call center's list.

Two replays, with the {{test_days}}-day run's surprises

The week is replayed twice: under Staple (skills and parts, balanced loads) and under the current rules (the nearest technician, today's stock). Both use seed {{dispatch_seed}} plus the index of the week's first day among all held-back days. So each day's sick calls, breakdowns and priority changes are the ones the {{test_days}}-day run met on the same day, and the plan check recounts them to prove it. The week starts from today's truck stock rather than the {{test_days}}-day run's last loads.

What is recorded

For each day: the night routes before {{sick_by}}, each truck's load and the load before it, each call's chosen technician with the runner-up and the cost gap, the {{sick_by}} sick calls, the breakdowns, the priority changes, every visit as the day ran with its start, end and whether the truck covered it, and the rolled calls. Recording changes no figure, and the dispatch check proves it on every policy. The day's figures are counted from the recorded visits exactly as the replay counts them, and the plan check holds them equal.

The first-time fix chance on the board

The board shades each call by its chance of a first-time fix: the truck must hold what the call needs, and the technician must then complete the repair.

{{EX_plan}}

A moved call is scored on the truck that took it, so its chance before and after shows what the move cost.

Where each carry-list part stands

The first that holds of: needs a certified technician, aboard, on a truck nearby (based within {{nearby}} minutes), at a center but not loaded, back-ordered.

The load sheet

A part on tonight's load and not the last is put on; on the last and not tonight's, taken off; on both, kept. Its worth is the balanced rule's own sum, split out as load.worth: each planned call's chance of needing it times {{repeat_visit}}, plus its share of unplanned calls, less its holding cost, with a {{move}} move counted against it when it is not aboard and for it when it is. The calls behind a part are those at least {{behind}} likely to need it, the three likeliest named.

The to-do rules

  • Parts room: one item per truck whose load changes, ordered by the worth of what goes on.
  • Call center: every booked call whose follow-up answer did not settle the code. A call that comes in during the day is on no list read before {{day_start}}.
  • Dispatch: every planned call with a part at least {{likely}} likely that is back-ordered with no truck nearby holding one, every call the night plan rolled, and one item naming the technicians with no booked calls.
  • Buyer: the top {{buyer_items}} parts on that day's back-order list, costliest first. Each item quotes the row backorder_exposure.sql returns for that day, so the plan and the evidence table show one figure.

The day the page opens on

The most recent of the {{week}} days on which a sick call and a breakdown both moved at least one call to another truck. A breakdown after a truck's last call strands its stock but moves nothing, so it does not count.

The metric queries

The checks and their controls

python check.py runs every check; python check.py <name> runs one. Each prints what it measured and fails the run on a broken rule. Where a check could pass without looking, it also feeds itself a planted input that must fail, because a check that cannot fail looks exactly like one that sees nothing. Corners cut on purpose

A corner is a simplification taken knowingly. Each is marked where the code cuts it by a comment that opens # ponytail:, which names the ceiling the corner sets and the upgrade that would lift it. This list is read from those comments at build time: {{corners_method}} in the method, and {{corners_checks}} in the checks and the build.
